# College Life 101

> College has a hidden operating system. This page is the quick-start manual.

## Quick Answer

- Read every syllabus during the first week.
- Put major deadlines into a calendar immediately.
- Use office hours before a class becomes a disaster.
- Email professors early when something starts going wrong.
- Deadlines for add/drop, withdrawal, and registration matter. Check the official current dates.

## First-Week Checklist

- [ ] Download or save each syllabus.
- [ ] Add exams, papers, projects, and registration dates to a calendar.
- [ ] Find each professor's office hours and contact method.
- [ ] Check whether each class uses Moodle, Google Drive, email, or another system.
- [ ] Make a backup plan for any class that already feels risky.

## Syllabus

**The syllabus is the rulebook for the class.**

Look for:

- grading,
- deadlines,
- attendance rules,
- late work rules,
- professor contact information,
- office hours.

If a policy is confusing, ask early. It is normal to ask for clarification.

## Office Hours

Office hours are time professors set aside for students.

You do not need to be failing. Good reasons to go:

- "Can I talk through this homework problem?"
- "Am I studying the right way for this class?"
- "What should I do if I am interested in research?"
- "Can you explain what you look for in a strong paper/project?"

## Emailing Professors

Use a simple format:

```text
Subject: Question about [course] assignment

Hi Professor [Name],

I am in [course/section]. I am working on [specific thing].

I tried [what you tried], but I am stuck on [specific problem].

Could you help me understand the next step, or should I come to office hours?

Thank you,
Your Name
```

## Falling Behind

First, take a breath. You're not failing. It happens. You're not letting anyone down. Missing one assignment is usually fixable. Ignoring the professor for three weeks makes it much harder to fix. So pause, get centered, and then make a plan. Start with *communicating*...

What to do:

- [ ] Email the professor.
- [ ] Say what is missing.
- [ ] Ask what can still be recovered.
- [ ] Make a short catch-up plan.
- [ ] Use tutoring, SQRC, or office hours.

If you're overwhelmed...call dad.

## Extensions

Ask before the deadline when possible. Professors are willing to work with a good student that just needs a little extra help. Remember that time that lady at the bar was drunk and told you that teaching sucks? And you said to me that most teachers *aren't* her? Most teachers care? Well...believe your own words. Teachers care. So talk to them when there is a problem. Don't assume the worst.

A good extension request is short:

- what happened,
- what you have already done,
- what new deadline you are asking for,
- whether you can submit partial work.

## Registration, Dropping, and Withdrawing

Registration involves advising, prerequisites, registration windows, waitlists, and backup classes.

Dropping and withdrawing are not the same thing. Deadlines can affect transcripts, financial aid, and progress toward graduation.

Use the [CAS Registrar](https://college.lclark.edu/offices/registrar/) and [undergraduate enrollment status rules](https://docs.lclark.edu/undergraduate/policiesprocedures/enrollmentstatus/) for current rules and dates.

## Academic Probation

Academic warning or probation means the college is concerned about academic progress.

It is not the end of college. It is a signal to get help quickly:

- advising,
- professor meetings,
- tutoring,
- accessibility support,
- counseling or health support if life stuff is affecting school.

## Study Time

College work often happens outside class.

Instead of using a rigid formula, ask:

- Do I understand the homework without looking at examples?
- Can I explain the idea out loud?
- Am I starting papers and projects before the last minute?
- Do I know what will be on the exam?

## Study Groups

Study groups are especially useful in math.

Good study groups do not just trade answers. They:

- compare approaches,
- explain ideas out loud,
- find mistakes earlier,
- make hard classes less lonely.
